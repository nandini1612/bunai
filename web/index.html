    About

    What you are looking at

    textiles made in India between roughly the 10th and 20th centuries, from the open-access collections of the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Cleveland Museum of Art, the Art Institute of Chicago and Cooper Hewitt. Every image is public domain (CC0).

    Likeness is computed, not curated. A vision model looked at each photograph and pieces that look alike were placed together. Nobody told it what a paisley is.

    Craft, region and colour names are automatic. A craft label is kept where the museum's record names the technique plainly. Where it does not, a model guesses from the photograph, and its guess is used only when it is confident: tested on pieces it had not seen, it was right about two times in three. Each piece says where its label came from. Colour names are the nearest traditional shade, and the dye notes describe how that shade was classically made, not a lab result for the piece.

    This is a partial picture. It shows what four American museums acquired, mostly in the 1800s and 1900s: court cloth, export goods and excavated fragments. Everyday cloth, and most of what India weaves today, is missing.

    Where the stories were checked

    Indian institutions and scholars come first. Museum catalogues are used for facts about particular objects, and peer-reviewed work for chemistry and legal history. Where a claim rests on colonial-era accounts, it says so.

    बुनाई · the act of weaving

    A thousand cloths, woven, printed and painted in India across a thousand years, now kept in museums far from the hands that made them.
